### Q: What should I do when the market is at an all time high?

Well, that's a question that gets right to the heart of how Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger think about investing, even if they don't often use the phrase "all-time high" directly. Their approach isn't about market timing, but about **value and pricing**.

Here's what we can glean from their wisdom:

1.  **Focus on Value, Not Market Levels:** Both Buffett and Munger consistently emphasize buying when you're "getting a lot for your money" (Buffett, 2009). Buffett likes it when prices are lower, comparing it to buying hamburgers on sale: "The cheaper they get, the better I like it." He clearly states, "Picking bottoms is basically not our game. Pricing is our game." This implies that an all-time high market might naturally offer fewer opportunities where you're "getting a lot for your money."

2.  **The Price of Entry Matters Immensely:** Buffett has explicitly said, "The price at which you enter is very important" (1997). Charlie Munger takes it a step further, cautioning that "Eventually, if the success continues and we have more of this hagiography, the stock will get to such a high price that it’s no longer sensible at all to buy" (1997). So, while they might hold onto great businesses even at high valuations (1996), they'd be very hesitant to *buy new ones* at those prices.

3.  **Reduced Expectations for Future Returns:** When the market reaches high valuations, Buffett and Munger warn that future returns are likely to be lower. In 1999, Buffett pointed out that corporate profits cannot grow faster than GDP indefinitely, calling the expectation of 15% annual equity growth "nonsense." Munger echoed this, stating, "The real long-term rate of return from saving money and investing it has to go down... We ought to have reduced expectations regarding the future, generally." He even quipped, "People who expect perpetual growth in real wealth in a finite earth are either mad men or economists" (1999).

4.  **Their Own Actions Speak Volumes:** When faced with high valuations, their actions often reflect caution. In 2005, Buffett noted that Berkshire owned securities they "probably wouldn't buy at these prices" and that a "lower percentage of our intrinsic value is represented by the common stocks we own than just about at any time of our history." He clarified that they weren't unhappy with their holdings, but they weren't buying more either, often being in a "zone where we would neither be a buyer nor a seller" due to size and tax implications. Munger acknowledged that it's harder for them to deploy large sums of money at satisfactory rates in such environments (2005).

5.  **For the Average Investor:** While they look for specific value, Buffett has suggested that for the average investor not actively involved in the process, "regular investment in some kind of very low-cost pool of money, which might well be an index fund, probably makes as much sense as anything," emphasizing the importance of keeping costs down (1999). This offers a pragmatic approach for those who aren't looking to pick individual stocks when opportunities are scarce.

In essence, while they don't tell you to sell everything at an all-time high, their advice leans towards **caution, reduced expectations for future returns, and a focus on finding truly attractive prices** – which are naturally harder to come by when the market is soaring. As Buffett would say, "Anytime we get a chance to do something that makes sense, we do it." The challenge at all-time highs is simply finding things that "make sense" at current prices.
