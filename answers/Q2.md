### Q: Will you split the stock?

Well, that's a question we've heard a few times over the years, and the short answer from both Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger has consistently been a resounding **no**.

As Warren put it in 1998, "We have no plans to split the A. In effect, we let people who want to split the A split it themselves into a B. So that anybody who owns the A can have a 30-for-1 split any morning they wake up and want to have such a split."

The core philosophy behind this decision, as both Warren and Charlie have explained, revolves around attracting and retaining a specific type of shareholder.

**From Warren Buffett's perspective:**

*   **A "Better Class of Shareholders":** He believes the high stock price acts as a self-selection mechanism, attracting "the best group of shareholders in the world" (2004). These are people who are more in sync with the company's policies, long-term in their view, and understand their investment better than shareholders of other large corporations.
*   **Lowest Turnover:** Berkshire has the lowest turnover of any large American corporation, which Warren attributes to this self-selection process (2004, 1995).
*   **Avoiding Speculators:** Splitting the stock would likely attract people who buy based on hype, temporary price boosts, or without a deep understanding of the business, rather than intrinsic value (1995). He doesn't want the stock price to be artificially stimulated; he wants it to parallel the growth of intrinsic value.
*   **Like a Private Partnership:** Warren wants Berkshire to feel as much like a private partnership as possible (1995). He argues that once you get a shareholder base with different objectives or expectations, you can't easily reconstruct the kind of group Berkshire currently enjoys.

**Charlie Munger is even more direct:**

*   He's called the idea of carving ownership into "little, tiny $20 pieces" almost "insane" (1994).
*   He also points out the inefficiency of servicing very small accounts.
*   Like Warren, Charlie frequently emphasizes the quality of the current shareholder base, often urging people to "look around this room" at the annual meeting and ask, "Are we really likely to do a lot better? This is a good bunch" (1994, 1995).

While Warren acknowledges that the high share price can be "awkward to disadvantageous" for things like gifting or mandatory IRA payments, he believes these are generally solvable issues and that the benefits of maintaining the current shareholder base far outweigh these inconveniences (1995).

So, in short, don't hold your breath for a stock split. They're quite happy with the partners they've got!
